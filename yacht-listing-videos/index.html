01 — Understanding

What you're solving for

A new brokerage launches with 225 listings, and every listing deserves a video that looks more expensive than the photos it came from. Buyers at this level know boats: a hull line that bends, a flybridge that grows a deck, or a saloon that changes colour between shots is worse than no video at all. A ten-second video is really three or four shots cut together, so the hard part is not one good clip. It is the same yacht surviving every cut, 225 times.

Video quality

"Clearly better than the input images, without looking artificial"

Listing photos get improved where it is safe: exposure, white balance, sky, water texture, noise and resolution. That is where "elevated" comes from. Nothing that would make a surveyor look twice gets generated.

Yacht accuracy

"The same vessel in every shot, matching the input images"

Every shot starts from one of that listing's photos, so the hull, colour, layout and key features come from the listing, not from a model's idea of a yacht. Each clip's first and last frames are checked against its source photo before it is cut in.

Stable, natural motion

"Smooth camera moves with believable water, reflections and people"

Slow, camera-led moves (push-in, lateral track, gentle orbit on the exterior) rather than the boat moving. Water and reflections are where AI gives itself away, so they are the first thing the review checks. People stay small and secondary, or out of frame when a listing photo has none.

Editing

"Good pacing and consistent colour across exterior, deck and interior shots"

One edit template for all 225: a fixed shot order and rhythm, one colour pipeline that matches interiors to exteriors, 1080p export, no sound. "Titles and labels are not needed", so none are added.

04 — Quality

Built so a buyer never spots the AI

01

Never text-to-video

Every shot starts from a real photo of that yacht. A model asked to imagine "a 24m flybridge" will give you a different boat; a model asked to move a camera across your photo gives you this one.

02

Improvement has a fence around it

Light, sky, water, noise and resolution can change. Hull, superstructure, window lines, deck hardware and interior layout cannot. That rule is enforced with masks, not left to a prompt.

03

Short moves, slow moves

Two to three seconds per shot, with the camera doing the moving. That is what keeps water, reflections and rigging stable; long or fast moves are where models start inventing.

04

People handled carefully

Faces and hands are where AI looks artificial fastest. People appear only where a listing photo has them, kept at a distance and in natural motion, unless you want them added on specific shots.

05

Attempt cap per listing

A shot that keeps failing stops after a set number of tries and gets a hands-on fix or a different source photo, so one awkward listing cannot burn the generation budget.

06

Yours, in your accounts

n8n workflows, ComfyUI graphs, model API keys, the Resolve template and the sheet all sit under your logins. Listing 226 runs the same way after February.

07 — The same pattern, elsewhere

Two worked examples of this architecture

Illustrative scenarios, not client case studies. They show the same building blocks, photo-anchored image-to-video, structure-locked improvement, an accuracy gate and one edit template, applied to other high-value listings.

Worked example · Prime property agency

Every new instruction gets a ten-second walk-through from the agent's photos

The agency lists forty homes a month, each with a photographer's set but no video budget. The pipeline picks a frontage, a living space and a garden shot, improves sky and light without touching the architecture, animates each with a slow push-in, and cuts them to the agency's house grade. A home's floor plan and window count are checked frame by frame, because a buyer who spots an extra window stops trusting the listing.

Worked example · Classic car dealer

Stock videos that keep the exact paint, wheels and badges of each car

A dealer with 150 cars in stock wants a short clip per car for the website and marketplaces. Each clip is cut from three photos, a three-quarter front, the interior and a detail, animated with a slow orbit and matched to one grade. Paint colour and wheel design are checked against the source photos; a clip where a badge softens is rejected before it reaches the dealer.
